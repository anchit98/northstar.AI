#### Your brain runs a separate circuit for dessert, and being full is what switches it on. Max Planck researchers stuffed mice with chow until they walked away from it, then offered sugar. Those already-full mice ate six times more calories than mice given more regular food. The satiety was the trigger. The cells doing it are POMC neurons in the hypothalamus, the same ones that release the signal telling you to stop eating. When sugar hits the tongue, those exact neurons also dump ß-endorphin, an opioid your own body manufactures, into the paraventricular thalamus. One cell population, two opposite orders, fired at the same moment. Timing is the strange part. The ß-endorphin released the instant the first sugar solution touched the mouth, before absorption, before a single calorie registered anywhere. It fired in mice that had never tasted sugar in their lives. Nothing learned, nothing conditioned. Factory setting. The pathway is picky too. Normal food did not trigger it. Fatty food did not trigger it. Sugar did. Block the opioid receptors and the full mice walked past the dessert entirely. Then they ran it on humans, sugar solution through a tube, scanner running. Same region activated, same dense field of opioid receptors sitting right next to the satiety neurons. Sugar stayed rare and calorically dense for almost all of mammalian history. A brain that stops at full leaves free energy on the table. So evolution wired the stop signal and the sugar override into the same cells, and the override wins. The dessert stomach is a real circuit, opioid driven, and it stays locked until dinner is over.

#### Two Berkeley economists once tracked 7,752 gym members for three years. Members on $70 monthly plans showed up 4.3 times a month, paying over $17 per visit while a $10 day pass sat at the front desk. The average member burned $600 on workouts that never happened. Arnold just built the first fitness business designed to hand that money back. The 2006 paper, "Paying Not to Go to the Gym," proved what every gym owner already knew. People buy memberships based on the person they plan to become, then bill the person they actually are. The industry priced that gap decades ago. January sells the dream, February collects from ghosts, and the ghosts subsidize cheap rates for everyone who shows up. Arnold flipped the direction of the payment. $100 for the app, $50 back when you finish your first program, $50 more when you finish your fourth. Behavioral economists have run this exact design in studies for 20 years. Deposit contracts, where your own money rides on your follow-through, beat willpower in nearly every trial. Smokers in one famous field experiment quit at higher rates just by putting cash on the line they'd lose for failing. So why did no gym chain ever ship it? Because their income statement runs on the ghosts. A refund for consistency attacks the exact revenue line that keeps the lights on. You need a business where the trained member is worth more than the absent one, and Arnold has that. Someone who completes four programs renews, refers friends, and buys from the store. The $100 refund is customer acquisition paid only to the customers who turn out to be worth the most. Gyms have spent 50 years cashing checks from people who stopped showing up. Arnold found the pricing model where the people who quit fund everyone who trains.

#### Hackers took a Minnesota town's water plant completely offline this week. The only thing that kept 1,700 people's taps running was the water tower, a technology from the 1880s that can't be hacked because it runs on gravity. Braham, Minnesota lost its well and its treatment plant when attackers remotely shut down the operating controls. For hours, the town survived on whatever water was already sitting in the tank. The entry point explains the whole story. Small water systems run their pumps and valves through programmable logic controllers, little industrial computers bolted to the equipment. To save on staffing, thousands of towns bought PLCs with cellular modems so one operator can manage everything from a phone. Minnesota's chief information security officer confirmed those cellular connections are exactly how the attackers got in. Same playbook as 2023, when Iranian-linked hackers breached a water authority in Aliquippa, Pennsylvania. The device they compromised was protected by the factory default password. It was 1111. America has roughly 50,000 community water systems, and most serve fewer than 3,300 people. These are utilities that can barely afford a second operator, let alone a security team. The FBI now counts incidents across at least seven states in a single week. And notice what the attackers didn't do. They never touched water quality. Knocking out remote management in 40+ towns at once looks less like sabotage and more like a mapping exercise: what breaks, how fast anyone notices, who has backups. The most secure layer of US water infrastructure turned out to be a steel tank on stilts. Everything below it is a computer with a SIM card.

Consumers funded the whole experiment through 18 months of higher prices. Now the refund checks are going to the warehouses.

#### Police need a warrant to read your private messages. Snapchat needed a law from 1986 to get a 22-year-old teacher arrested over one, in under an hour, without a single human reading it first. The mechanism matters because it applies to every app on your phone. The Fourth Amendment stops the government from reading your messages without a warrant. It says nothing about a company reading them. The Electronic Communications Privacy Act, passed in 1986 when "provider" meant a guy running an email server, includes an emergency exception. A provider that believes in good faith there is danger of death or serious injury may hand your messages to law enforcement voluntarily. Courts almost never second-guess good faith. The law even shields the company from being sued for the disclosure. In 1986, "good faith belief" meant a human judgment call. In 2026, it means a classifier's confidence score crossing a threshold.
