#### Harry Truman became the face of Mount St. Helens for refusing to leave. The forgotten stat from that eruption points the blame somewhere else entirely. Of the 57 people the volcano killed on May 18, 1980, only 3 died inside the official Red Zone. All 3 had permission to be there. The other 54 died on land the state of Washington had labeled safe. Weyerhaeuser was logging old growth timber on the mountain's north flank, and Governor Dixy Lee Ray drew her restricted zone right where the company's land began. Hundreds of loggers worked within a few miles of a north face that was bulging 5 feet per day. The eruption came on a Sunday. On a Monday morning, the death toll would have been in the hundreds, every one of them there legally. Even the USGS lost its own man. Volcanologist David Johnston died at an observation post 6 miles out, a site the agency considered safe until the mountain's entire north flank collapsed in the largest landslide in recorded history and the lateral blast ran 17 miles. A request to expand the restricted zone was sitting unsigned on the governor's desk that morning. After the eruption, Ray told reporters the dead had been trespassing. President Carter repeated it on national TV, and the story stuck for 45 years. The stubborn old man with 16 cats made a better villain than a map drawn around timber revenue.

#### Warner Bros spent $290 million making and marketing Supergirl. It will finish around $130 million worldwide. That's less than The Marvels, the movie Hollywood spent the last two years treating as the definition of a superhero bomb. The revealing part is when the check got written. Supergirl was greenlit and fully shot before Superman ever opened in theaters. DC committed $170 million to chapter two of a universe before a single ticket buyer had voted on chapter one. Then Superman worked. $643 million, 90% on Rotten Tomatoes. And the follow-up still cratered 74% in its second weekend, because audiences showed up for Superman, and a shared universe treated that as a subscription. The production math forces this bet. A tentpole takes 2 to 3 years from greenlight to release, so studios wager nine figures on the sequel while the original is still in post. When the gamble hits, executives look like visionaries. When it misses, you get a movie that needed $425 million to break even fighting to reach $130 million. The comparison that should terrify every studio came from the same release window. Young Washington, a $20 million film about George Washington's early life, averaged $7,721 per theater. Supergirl averaged $2,665. The indie history movie tripled the superhero tentpole per screen. Warner Bros will eat a loss between $80 and $120 million on this one film. The universe model assumed the brand carries the character. Twenty years of box office data now says it runs the other way.

#### A rabbit cannot see what is directly in front of its nose. Eyes high on the sides of the skull buy almost 360 degrees of coverage, and the price is a blind spot about 10 degrees wide pointing dead ahead. The one direction it sprints is the one direction it cannot see. Depth perception is worse. Binocular overlap runs 10 to 30 degrees, against roughly 120 in a human. To judge distance a rabbit bobs its head side to side and reads the parallax. That takes time. At 35 mph it has none. So it runs the only algorithm it has. Bolt, then cut hard across the pursuer's line at the last possible moment. Against a fox that is close to unbeatable, because every cut forces the fox to plant a foot, rotate its entire body and reaccelerate, dropping a body length each time. The maneuver is scored on one variable only, how expensive it is for the thing behind you to turn. A scooter is not behind it. A scooter passes through at constant speed on a fixed line with no interest in the rabbit whatsoever. The cut that would have shaken a fox lands directly in the path instead of out of it. Then the physics turn unfair. Rabbit, call it 2kg. Rider and machine, call it 250. Momentum says nothing should happen here. What matters is where the 2kg arrives. A two wheeler holds itself upright through continuous micro corrections at the front contact patch, so any lateral force on the fork or front wheel registers as a steering command. The bike obeys it. And a leaping rabbit clears the ground, which puts it at fork height rather than under the tire. Reaction time to a fully unexpected event sits near 250 milliseconds. At 35 mph that is 13 feet of road gone before your hands know anything happened. The lean angle is already chosen. Timing is the last piece. Rabbits are crepuscular, activity peaking at dawn and dusk, which is exactly where that sun is sitting in the frame. The window for this is about two hours a day. An escape system evolved to punish anything that has to turn, executed against something that was never going to turn.

#### Vizio lost $6.7 million selling TVs in its final quarter as a public company. Its ads and data division made $115.8 million in the same three months. Your TV samples the pixels on screen, matches them against a database of everything airing anywhere, and builds a second-by-second log of what you watch. The FTC found Vizio's system pulling up to 100 billion data points a day from 11 million TVs, then selling the logs to brokers who matched them to your IP address, your phone, and your purchase history. Roku sells hardware below cost. Samsung and LG built their own ad networks. The cheaper the TV, the more living rooms the software reaches, so pricing pressure runs downward on purpose. Walmart paid $2.3 billion for Vizio in December 2024, and the announcement led with the advertising business. The world's largest retailer wanted to pair what you watch at 9pm with what you buy at 9am. This is also why a "dumb" commercial display with no apps costs more than the smart version of the same panel. The dumb one has to earn its whole profit on the shelf. The smart one earns for a decade after you carry it home.

#### My biggest takeaways from @tdrobbo (CPO at @Whatnot): 1. AI’s biggest unlock for PMs now is in data science (not prototyping). Cohort retention pulls, analyzing user logs, and sensitivity and regression models on a @_hex_tech thread—work that took an Amazon L7 data scientist a week or two in 2017 can be done in minutes. Tom spends 10x more time in data than at any point in his career and less time talking to data scientists than ever. 2. Tom “regrets that product management exists,” but this isn't an insult to PMs. Tom argues that PM is a trade, not a qualification—a muscle built by reps. Hiring too many PMs as a default takes reps away from engineers and designers, infantilizing people perfectly capable of good decisions who never had to make them because “there was always a PM to babysit them.” 3. AI is exposing rampant “product theater.” Stakeholder management, alignment, storytelling—real work, but not product work, and they’re what the industry has rewarded PMs for the past five years. But theater only survives where real work is expensive to verify, and it isn't anymore. Every Whatnot hire is given a problem and a dataset and defends a POV out loud — and you watch how quickly the thinking decays in people who can present but can't think clearly. 4. The PM skill trending up fastest: systems thinking. Tom asks this question in every product review: “What do we do if the experiment is green? What do we do if it’s red?” If the answer is “I don’t know,” you haven't done the mental work necessary to run this experiment. 5. Stop promoting your A-players out of the work they’re great at. The common trap: take an A-player PM, make them a director, tell them to stop being hands-on, watch their instincts atrophy while junior PMs yo-yo the same PRD through review. At Whatnot, the four or five PM managers each spend at least 90% of their time on IC work; Tom spends about half his time as CPO as an IC. 6. “It’s really complex” is usually code for weak leadership. Twitter ran working groups on lifting the 140-character limit for Tom’s entire tenure. Everyone knew it was inevitable—Japanese users tweeted six times as often because kanji packs more meaning per character—but nobody would make the call. It shipped almost two years after he left, and nothing bad happened. When something seems complicated, ask whether it’s actually complicated or whether leadership just won’t decide. 7. Map PMs to problems, not teams. Every six months, Tom, the CEO, and senior leads define the company strategy and key pillars, then assign DRIs by skill set. Some engineering teams go a year without a PM. The result: a PM who’s worked on buyer, seller, and trust problems instead of spending three years on notifications. More reps, more muscle groups. 8. Averages mean nothing to the individual. This is the thread running through Tom’s worst decisions. A feature only 3% of users touch looks like a deprecation candidate—until you go a layer deeper and find that for some users it’s 100% of their use case, and killing it is like “a Westfield mall turning off the power in the lead-up to Christmas.” Always ask what distribution hides under the average, and when the anecdote conflicts with the data, trust the anecdote. 9. True PMF is something you feel before you see it in the data. Twitter had nine heads of product in the two years Tom was there and survived anyway, because the product had a level of PMF that absorbed extraordinary mismanagement. Elon changed the name, brand, team, and culture; the network effects held. The career lesson: work somewhere with true PMF early so you learn what bottled lightning actually feels like. 10. End turf wars by giving one person both sides of the tradeoff. At Twitch, discovery and ads were perpetually at war for impressions. One of Tom’s first moves was to put ads inside discovery under a single PM accountable for total GMV from the feed, organic and paid alike. The tradeoff gets made naturally, cutting months of back-and-forth and the politics that turn decisions from company-first to career-first.

One of the arguments for open source software for end-users has always been the freedom to examine and modify how that software works. The reality for most people - even expert programmers - has been that the freedom is more about being able to lean on other people to do that. Most people can't justify the time commitment needed to read and then modify the code for tools they use very often.
