#### The final hour of sleep before you actually get up is the most valuable hour your brain gets all night. Five alarms between 5:30 and 8am destroy it. Sleep runs in cycles of about 90 minutes. Deep slow wave sleep front loads into the first half of the night. REM back loads into the second half. Your first REM period runs roughly 10 minutes. Your last one can run close to an hour. That final REM block is where emotional memory gets consolidated, where your brain reprocesses yesterday's stress with the noradrenaline switched off. Nowhere else in the 24 hour day does that happen. Waking makes it worse. Blood flow does not return to your brain evenly when you come out of sleep. Brainstem and thalamus fire back fast. Your prefrontal cortex, the region running judgment, focus, and impulse control, is the last to reperfuse, and it takes roughly 20 to 30 minutes. That lag is sleep inertia. A real physiological state, not a personality trait. Five alarms trigger it five times. Each one drops you into N1, the shallow drowsy stage sitting right below wakefulness. The next alarm fires before you descend anywhere useful. You spend 150 minutes surfing the top of sleep, restarting the inertia clock over and over, never rebuilding a full cycle. A Hiroshima University lab study using repeated snooze alarms found exactly that, the forced re awakenings prolonged grogginess instead of relieving it. Short snoozing does the opposite. Stockholm University ran a polysomnography study in 2023 and found 30 minutes of snoozing cost about six minutes of real sleep, left mood flat, and slightly improved cognitive scores on waking. One or two taps keeps you from getting ripped out of slow wave sleep. That's the whole benefit, and it holds up. Thirty minutes helps. Two and a half hours wrecks the mechanism. You set five alarms to get more sleep. Your brain filed those two hours as five separate mornings and charged you for all of them.

#### Cancer cells make themselves vulnerable to Viagra. They dial down the exact machinery the drug jams, because carrying less of it lets them grow faster. Sildenafil blocks PDE5, which lets cGMP build up inside the cell. The blood flow effect everyone knows about comes from cGMP relaxing smooth muscle. The Weizmann team found it has a second job. cGMP binds NPC1, the transporter that ships cholesterol out of the lysosome and into the rest of the cell. Jam NPC1 and the cholesterol gets stranded. The cell is full of it and starving for it at the same time. Cholesterol is the stiffening agent in a cell membrane. A tumor cell that wants to metastasize has to detach, squeeze through tissue, survive the bloodstream, and rebuild itself somewhere new. Every one of those steps is membrane remodeling. Lipid rafts fall apart without available cholesterol. Mitochondrial output drops with them. A cell that can't manage its own membranes stays where it is. Healthy cells shrug this off. Tumor cells have reduced lysosomal gene expression, the way a startup cuts the maintenance team to ship faster, so they have almost no slack in the system sildenafil interferes with. What makes a tumor aggressive is what makes it sensitive here. Then the cell fights back. The shortage triggers SREBP2, which switches on synthesis of new cholesterol from scratch. That escape hatch already has a drug sitting on it. Statins. Sildenafil traps the cholesterol you have, statins stop you making more, and in the mouse models the pair hit harder than either alone. The human side is 20 years of records on 5 million Clalit members in Israel, where cancer patients taking sildenafil survived longer, and those on sildenafil plus statins did better still. Retrospective data, confounding is live, nobody should act on this. But both drugs are off patent and cost pocket change, so no company has a reason to fund the trial that would settle it. The most interesting oncology result of the summer runs on two of the cheapest pills in the pharmacy.
