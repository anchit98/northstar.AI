#### Kevin O'Leary's newly approved Utah data center will pump out 16 gigawatts of heat. That's roughly one hair dryer running full blast for every household in California, all pointed at a single desert valley, 24 hours a day. The heat comes from both ends of the operation. The gas turbines powering it waste 7 gigawatts just making the electricity. The chips turn the other 9 into heat too, because a processor converts every watt it draws into warmth. The computation rides out on the exhaust. A Utah State physics professor named Rob Davies ran the numbers and found nighttime temperatures in the valley could climb as much as 28°F. That's enough to stop dew from forming. In high desert, dew is a water supply. Every plant and animal that drinks the night air loses it permanently. The project covers the footprint of Washington D.C. It will consume more than double the electricity used by the entire state of Utah, plus an estimated 16.6 billion gallons of water a year, pulled near a Great Salt Lake already at record lows. And because it generates its own power, it skipped utility review entirely. Approval ran through a military development authority created to protect an Air Force base, which cut the project's property taxes by 80%. The county commissioners voted yes unanimously, after leaving the public meeting for a private room.

#### If a woman in stilettos steps on your foot, she does more damage per square inch than an elephant would. Roughly 1,600 pounds of pressure versus 75. Even the chunky block heels the QVC host is wearing land at around double an elephant, and she's stepping around on an inflatable mattress in them, live on air. Pressure is force divided by area. An elephant spreads its weight across four feet the size of dinner plates. A heel concentrates an entire body onto a patch smaller than a dime. This is why heels sink into grass, dent hardwood, and get banned from gym floors. So stepping across an air mattress in heels is one of the harshest blunt-force tests you can run on the material. Per square inch, it's harsher than parking a car on it. Kevlar vests survive bullets with the same trick the mattress is about to use. Spread a point load across thousands of fibers faster than the point can cut through. What everyone's laughing at is soft armor physics on daytime TV. The mattress survives because of how it fails to resist. The air inside is only pressurized to about 1 psi. When the heel lands, the surface deflects, wraps around it, and spreads the load across a patch of fabric dozens of times wider than the heel itself. In milliseconds, the pressure collapses into a number the laminate handles easily. An actual puncture needs a sharp edge that severs fibers, or a rigid backing that stops the material from stretching out of the way. A blunt heel on a yielding surface has neither. The mattress converts a stabbing problem into a stretching problem, and woven fabric is spectacular at stretching problems. The heel comes down harder than an elephant's foot. The air pushing back is at 1 psi. The mattress wins anyway.

#### SpaceX went public at $1.75 trillion in June. Yesterday it committed $16.8 billion to a chip fab in Grimes County, Texas. Follow the chain connecting those two numbers and you end at a light source that doesn't exist yet. Start from the top. SpaceX has filed for up to 1 million data-center satellites, targeting hundreds of gigawatts of compute in orbit. Musk says current global chip output covers about 3% of what his companies will eventually need. So Terafab exists to print over 1 terawatt of compute per year across 100 million square feet, the largest chip site ever attempted. Here's where the physics bites. Every advanced chip gets patterned with EUV light, and today that light comes from vaporizing 50,000 molten tin droplets per second with a laser inside each $200 million scanner. The tin splatters, the power caps out, and every scanner needs its own source. A free-electron laser flips the architecture. Shoot electrons through undulator magnets and you get roughly 4x the EUV power with no tin debris. One accelerator can feed up to 20 scanners for 30 years, cutting per-wafer costs by about half. That math is mediocre for TSMC, which spreads fabs across dozens of sites. A single 100 million square foot campus is the one place on Earth where a central light plant amortizes perfectly. Terafab's weird design choice and the FEL's weird economics fit each other like a key in a lock. The pieces are already moving. Pat Gelsinger chairs xLight, which just took a $150 million CHIPS award to build the first FEL prototype in Albany by 2028. ASML's CEO confirmed Musk is in direct talks about Terafab. Starlink pays the bills. Starship gets the applause. The number that decides whether SPCX holds $2 trillion is photons per second at 13.5 nanometers.
