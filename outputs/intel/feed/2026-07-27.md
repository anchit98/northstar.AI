Elon is probably right that AI and robots will outproduce human consumption of manufactured goods. Keynes was right about that too, 96 years early. Money survived 1930's abundance for one reason. Humans kept finding new things to run out of.

#### The busiest day in aviation history just happened while passenger demand is shrinking. 153,359 flights on July 23, smashing the old record of 139,237. Weeks earlier, IATA reported global demand fell 2.2% in May, the second straight monthly drop. Airlines are flying record schedules into a softening market, and the reason is a rule most flyers have never heard of. At the world's congested airports, airlines don't own their takeoff times. They hold slots, governed by a use-it-or-lose-it rule. Fly at least 80% of your slots each season or regulators hand them to a competitor. Permanently. These slots trade like Manhattan real estate. Oman Air paid Air France-KLM $75 million in 2016 for a single pair at Heathrow. One takeoff and one landing per day. You saw what this incentive does during COVID. Lufthansa admitted it would fly 18,000 nearly empty flights in one winter purely to keep its slots. Burning fuel to carry nobody was cheaper than surrendering the asset. Same logic runs today. Canceling a flight saves fuel and crew costs once. Giving up a Heathrow or Haneda slot costs eight figures to reverse, if it can be reversed at all. The math says fly, every time. To be fair, part of the demand drop is the Middle East conflict, where traffic fell 28%. Strip that out and demand is roughly flat. But flat demand with record departures still tells you what airlines actually optimize for. Every yellow plane on that map is an airline defending territory it paid millions to hold. When your parking spot costs $75 million, you fly whether the passengers show up or not.

#### A meta-analysis of 98,299 people just found that heavy short-form video use is linked to weakened performance in the exact brain function you'd need to stop watching short-form video. Researchers at Griffith University pooled 71 studies on TikTok, Reels, and Shorts users. Overall cognition showed a moderate negative association (r = -.34). But the single strongest effect in the entire dataset was inhibitory control, at r = -.41. Stronger than anxiety. Stronger than stress. Inhibitory control is your prefrontal cortex's brake pedal. It fires when you resist checking your phone mid-conversation, when you sit through a slow chapter, when you stay with a hard problem past the point of discomfort. Like a muscle, it strengthens through reps. And a rep, neurologically, is any moment you feel the urge to escape and don't. The swipe deletes the rep. Every other medium in history forced you through boring stretches. A film's setup, a lecture's dry middle, an album's weaker track. Short-form video resolves boredom in under a second, thousands of times a day, using your thumb instead of your prefrontal cortex. So the loop closes on itself. Weaker brakes make it harder to stop scrolling. More scrolling means fewer reps. Fewer reps mean weaker brakes. The correlation runs both directions, and both directions point down. The strangest finding got buried. SFV use showed no association with body image or self-esteem, the two harms everyone assumed. The measurable damage shows up in attention and impulse control, the harms nobody was watching for. Every boring moment you swipe away is a rep your prefrontal cortex didn't get.

#### A 0.7 lead carries 96% more graphite in its cross-section than a 0.5. That single number explains the whole schoolyard divide. Lead strength scales with cross-sectional area, so a 0.7 survives roughly double the writing pressure before it snaps. Team 0.5 was kids with a light touch who wanted the crisp thin line. Team 0.7 was kids who pressed hard, wrote fast, and had learned through breakage what their hands could get away with. Team 0.5 vs team 0.7 was a handwriting pressure test disguised as a preference. The debate is also younger than people assume. Before 1962 it could not exist. Pencil lead was graphite bound with clay, and clay lead snapped instantly below 0.9mm. Nobody on earth could manufacture a usable 0.5. Pentel cracked it by swapping clay for synthetic resin. Heat the resin and it carbonizes, fusing with the graphite into a lead far stronger, darker, and smoother than clay ever allowed. Company lore says founder Yukio Horie got the idea watching food char in a pan. The first polymer lead shipped in 1960 at 0.9mm. The 0.5 and 0.7 launched together in 1962, twins from the same breakthrough. Both tubes in this photo are Pentel Hi-Polymer. Whichever team you were on, you were holding the same accident.
