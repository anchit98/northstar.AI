#### Guinness wrote down every recipe it ever brewed, going back to 1759. The company still can't recreate its own 1864 beer. The missing ingredient just turned up in a shipwreck at the bottom of the English Channel. Here's the problem paper can't solve. A recipe tells you the grain, the hops, the water. It can't store the yeast, and yeast does most of the work of making a beer taste like itself. Which is why microbiologists at KU Leuven want this bottle so badly. It sat upside down on the wreck of the Mindora, 5km off Dover, for 162 years. The trapped air bubble pressurized the cork like a seal, and the cold dark seabed made a better cellar than anything the Victorians built on land. Living yeast cells probably didn't survive. Doesn't matter. Dead cells still carry a full genome. Sequence it, match it against thousands of archived strains, and you can brew what the lead geneticist calls a "modern sibling" of the 1864 original. There's precedent. Divers pulled 168-year-old champagne from a Baltic wreck in 2010. Still drinkable. Bottles later sold for €30,000. A Finnish brewery rebrewed an 1840s beer the same way, from microbes left inside shipwreck bottles. My favorite part is the inversion. Diageo owns Guinness and 260 years of company records. Yet the closest anyone has come to tasting 1864 Guinness is a Belgian dive photographer who rinsed sand off a bottle and saw "Extra Stout" on the glass. Some things can't be backed up on paper. You have to find them at the bottom of the sea.

#### A doctor in Japan ate lightly seared chicken, had a week of diarrhea, and is now in a wheelchair. The reason is that Campylobacter, the bacteria in most raw chicken, wears a sugar coat copied almost molecule for molecule from the surface of human motor nerves. About two thirds of Campylobacter jejuni strains carry the copy. The sugar on their outer membrane matches GM1, a ganglioside concentrated where your nerves meet your muscles. Your immune system cannot tell the forgery from the original. So it builds antibodies against the bacterial coat, clears the infection, and keeps hunting the same signature. It finds it on your own nerve fibers. One to two weeks after the diarrhea ends, the antibodies start stripping the wiring. Weakness begins in the feet and climbs. In severe cases it reaches the diaphragm and the patient goes on a ventilator. This is Guillain-Barré syndrome, and Campylobacter is its most common trigger worldwide. The odds per infection are about 1 in 1,000. That sounds reassuring until you see the denominator. Japan officially records around 2,000 to 3,000 Campylobacter poisonings a year. When researchers ran active surveillance in 2006, the real number came out near 1.5 million. The official count was low by a factor of 450, because a few days of diarrhea rarely gets reported to anyone. Toriwasa makes the math worse by design. The dish is seared on the outside and raw at the center, and Campylobacter dies at 65°C at the center of the meat. It keeps the one layer that matters below the one temperature that matters. The man in the wheelchair spent his career warning patients about preventable risks. Japan is now writing its first nationwide hygiene rules for raw chicken dishes. The antibodies that took his legs were manufactured by his own body, doing exactly what it was built to do.

#### In 1972, an Italian who couldn't speak English wrote a No. 1 hit in fake English. The only real words in the entire song are "all right." Your brain classifies a language from rhythm and sound alone, before comprehension ever kicks in. Newborns just days old can tell two languages apart purely by rhythm. So he reverse engineered the rhythm of American English. English is stress-timed. The beat lands on stressed syllables and the vowels in between get crushed into "uh." He bottled the exact experience every non-English speaker has listening to American radio. And he improvised the whole thing freestyle over a looped beat, chanting verses against a call-and-response chorus. In 1972. Seven years before Rapper's Delight. An Italian prank about American music accidentally recorded a blueprint of rap. Fifty-three years later, Spike Lee commissioned a custom version from the 87-year-old Celentano for his film. The joke became a genre, then a legacy.
