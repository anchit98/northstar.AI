#### An electric SUV drove a full loop across a tunnel ceiling at 130 km/h, and the margin for error was 3 to 5 km/h. Slow down by that much at the top and the car falls on its roof. We know because a leaked video of a failed practice run shows exactly that happening. The physics is weirder than the stunt. The critical speed for a vertical loop comes from v² = gr, and mass cancels out of the equation. A five-meter family SUV needs the same entry speed in that tunnel as a supercar would. Extra weight buys nothing and costs nothing on speed. Where weight sends the bill is structure. At the bottom of the loop the tires and suspension carry several times the vehicle's weight. At the top, a battery pack weighing hundreds of kilograms hangs inverted off mounts engineered for the opposite load direction. Voyah says the car ran with its full pack and driver-assist hardware installed, zero weight reduction. The stunt is a battery-retention test wearing a marketing budget. The precision requirement reads like a typo. Enter at 130 km/h with the steering held at exactly 16 degrees, then keep both numbers steady while the horizon rotates around you. Mercedes filmed the same stunt with Michael Schumacher and an SLS AMG in 2010, and people have spent 16 years arguing about whether it was real. Voyah answered its own skeptics differently. At the car's launch two days later, they released the entire run as a single uncut take. The car starts at $33,800. And the strongest proof the stunt was real is footage of it failing.

#### A dog's nose has around 300 million scent receptors. Yours has about 6 million. In a 1,502-person trial in Bangalore, trained dogs detected early-stage cancer from breath samples with roughly 90% sensitivity. Then they did something nobody trained them to do. Tumors rewire cell metabolism, and that altered metabolism leaks volatile organic compounds into your blood, which diffuse into your lungs and exit in your breath. The concentrations are parts per trillion. Roughly one drop of scent in 20 Olympic pools. No commercial breath machine reads reliably at that level. A dog does it in seconds. Dogs have flagged disease in over 50 peer-reviewed studies across 28 conditions. The reason none of it ever reached a clinic is that a dog isn't a repeatable instrument. Two handlers read the same sniff differently. So the startup, Dognosis, strapped sensors to the dogs, tracking movement, respiration, and brain activity, and trained an AI to translate the animal into standardized clinical output. The AI doesn't smell anything. It reads the dog. Then the part that should make researchers sit up. Dogs trained on 10 cancer types correctly flagged an 11th type they had never been shown. They generalized. That implies cancer may have a shared chemical signature across tumor types, one no lab has isolated, that a beagle named Whiskey can pick out of a breath mask. The Phase 2 results were published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology, though the headline figures are still company-reported. A 10,000-person Phase 3 trial is now underway. The at-home version targets a 2027 launch in India at a few thousand rupees per test, breathe into a mask for 10 minutes, courier it to a farm outside Bangalore, and a dog clocks in for its shift. We sequenced the human genome for $300. The most sensitive cancer detector on the planet still works for kibble.

#### A parked airliner has no air conditioning of its own. Cooling comes from the engines, and with engines off, from a small jet turbine in the tail called the APU that burns about 110 kg of fuel an hour, roughly $90 at today's prices. Airlines track those minutes obsessively, because the APU also racks up maintenance hours toward overhauls that cost hundreds of thousands of dollars. On the ground, cool air is a line item. The physics on the other side of that line item are unforgiving. A resting human body gives off about 100 watts of heat. Ryanair's 737s seat 189 people, which makes a full cabin a 19-kilowatt heater, the output of a dozen space heaters running inside a sealed metal tube. Add Mediterranean sun on the fuselage and an unpowered cabin climbs past the outside temperature and keeps going. Passengers on Monday's Malta to Birmingham flight sat in that for over an hour, after already spending an hour split between two buses with failing air conditioning. Two people passed out. Others vomited in their seats. Now the number worth sitting with. Running the APU flat out for the entire 80-minute delay burns about $120 of fuel. Split across 189 passengers, keeping every single person on that plane cool costs roughly 60 cents a head. Maybe the technical issue that grounded the flight was the cooling system itself. Planes break. But according to passengers, nothing was broken on the drinks cart. Water still had a price, including for the people having medical episodes in the aisle.

#### Chicago officially renamed the Sears Tower in 2009. Seventeen years later, locals still call it the Sears Tower, the company that bought the naming rights has come and gone from the building, and cab drivers correct tourists who say Willis. Linguists who study imposed renames have documented this pattern for a century. Names belong to the people who say them. The mechanism is coordination. A name's only job is to be recognized by the listener, so every speaker rationally uses whichever version the most people already know. That makes an established name self-reinforcing, and it means a new name can't win on preference. It needs institutional lock-in, points in daily life where the old name literally stops working, and the history of successful renames is really a history of governments building those chokepoints on purpose. Istanbul is the cleanest case. The name became official in 1930, and adoption still dragged until the Turkish postal service started returning any mail addressed to Constantinople. Mumbai had airline codes, tickets, and government forms doing the same enforcement job. The rename won because ordinary transactions failed under the old name. Twitter has zero enforcement points. Nothing anyone does on the platform requires saying, typing, or addressing its name. Tweets still send if you call it Twitter. So the swap happened in the app store and nowhere in speech, and 17 years of a name that means something beat 3 years of a letter that collides with half the alphabet's other uses. Turkey needed the postal service to kill Constantinople. A rebrand with no post office behind it is a suggestion.

#### The 2011 Green Lantern suit destroyed a $300 million investment, and the reason it existed is the same reason fans hated it. In the comics, a Lantern's suit is a construct of light generated by the ring. Warner Bros decided fabric would be a betrayal of the source material. So Ryan Reynolds shot the entire movie in a gray motion-capture suit, and Sony Imageworks painted the costume on afterward, frame by frame, across 1,300 VFX shots. Animators simulated his muscle volumes moving under the surface so the suit would read as living energy. Audiences read it as skin.
