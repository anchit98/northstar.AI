#### In 1932, with breadlines in every city and unemployment at 25%, American workers took home 51.5% of national income. In 2026, with the stock market at record highs, they take home 43%. That comparison sounds backwards until you see how the accounting works. Wage share is countercyclical. When the economy crashes, profits collapse first and fastest while paychecks stay sticky. The Great Depression handed workers their largest slice in history, peaking at 52.6% in 1944, because there was barely any profit left to count. Run that logic forward and today's number reads differently. A record-low wage share means profits are eating a record portion of the pie. Corporate profits now claim 12.1% of national income, the highest since 1950. Fortune 500 companies booked $1.87 trillion in 2024, an all-time record. Since the end of 2019, inflation-adjusted corporate profits grew 50%. Inflation-adjusted hourly wages grew 3%. Part of the wage gap moved into benefits, mostly employer health premiums, which is why economists prefer total compensation. Even that measure fell to 51% of national income this year, the lowest since the series began in 1947. This is the entire explanation for why GDP prints look great and voters say the economy is terrible. Both are reading the same report. They're just reading different lines.

#### Two beers makes you about 38% better at one specific kind of puzzle. That number comes from a lab, not a bar. Sober subjects solved 42% of the items. At a blood alcohol of 0.075 they solved 58%, and got there in 11.5 seconds instead of 15.2. Here is the machinery. Alcohol binds GABA-A receptors, your brain's main braking system, and the prefrontal cortex has some of the densest braking in the skull. That region runs your attention filter. Its whole job is holding you on the thing you decided to focus on and throwing out everything drifting in from the sides. Puzzles like the one they used need the sides. You get three words, cottage, Swiss, cake, and you find the one that links them. Cheese. You cannot march to that answer in a straight line. It arrives from the periphery, and a sharp filter is exactly what keeps it out. Loosen the filter and the stray connections walk in. The same subjects scored worse on working memory in that session. The gain came from the loss. Dopamine handles the charisma side. Alcohol pushes release in the nucleus accumbens on the way up, which is the same circuit that makes a first bite of dessert land. Approach behavior goes up. Talking to strangers stops feeling like a cost. Then the part worth sitting with. Your anterior cingulate cortex fires a spike about 50 milliseconds after you make a mistake, before you consciously know you made one. That spike is the flinch. EEG studies at moderate doses show it flattening. People kept making the same number of errors and stopped correcting after them. So a real piece of that clarity is the alarm going quiet. You feel sharper because the circuit that tells you you're wrong dims faster than the circuit doing the thinking. Alcohol is biphasic, which is why nobody can park in the good window. Stimulation while blood alcohol is climbing, sedation while it falls. The identical 0.06 reads as charisma on the way up and as heaviness on the way down. Your body is tracking the direction, not the number. The window opens on the climb and closes at the peak. The next drink cannot put you back in it. What you were standing on was the slope.

#### The teams I see picking cheaper models to save money on coding agents are often the ones spending more. Inference spend is up something like 10x as agents scaled, and downgrading made it worse for a lot of them. A weaker model stalls and retries. Or it creates work a stronger model has to fix later. And switching models mid-session means the new model can't read the warm cache, so you're paying full price to re-read context you'd already paid for. The cheapest call is rarely the cheapest way to complete the task. That's why most of the cost-cutting moves I see fail. Usage caps and forced downgrades do save money, but mostly by making the output worse. Routers that only look at the next prompt can switch models at the wrong time and blow up a warm cache, which ends up costing more. What caught my attention in the Not Diamond launch is that it optimizes the whole session. It decides per turn how hard to think and on which model, and the cache is part of that math. Presumably that sometimes means staying on the expensive model, since breaking cache would cost more, which feels backwards until you add up the whole session. Their claim is frontier-level quality at 20%+ lower inference cost, and session-level math is why a number like that is even plausible. Zoom out and models churn every few months, so the layer deciding which one to use at each step probably outlasts any single model. Hard to see teams still hand-picking one default model for everything a couple years from now.

#### A solar eclipse next August will last 6 minutes and 23 seconds. The one Americans drove hours to see in 2024 peaked at 4 minutes 28 seconds. Earth sits near aphelion in early August, its farthest point from the sun, so the sun appears about as small as it ever looks. The moon, meanwhile, is near perigee, its closest pass to Earth, so it appears about as large as it ever looks. A slightly bigger disk covering a slightly smaller disk stays covered longer. The second is shadow width. That size mismatch fattens the moon's umbra to roughly 258 km across at maximum, one of the widest of the century. A wider shadow simply takes longer to sweep past you. The third is the one almost nobody knows. The moon's shadow crosses space at about 2,300 mph. Near the equator, the ground underneath you is literally chasing the shadow, cutting its speed overhead nearly in half. The 2027 path runs across Egypt at low latitude, so Luxor gets the full effect. #### A caterpillar that learns to fear a smell three weeks before it dissolves forgets it completely. The moths that had been trained in their fifth instar, the final stage before the body liquefies, still avoided the odor a month later. The ones trained at third instar did not, even though those same animals had visibly learned it as larvae and still remembered it two molts later. The gap is a construction schedule. Odor memory in insects lives in the mushroom bodies, which get built in stages. The gamma lobe forms in the embryo. The alpha/beta lobe doesn't begin until pupation starts. When the caterpillar breaks down, the early neurons get pruned back to the trunk and rewired for a body that flies. The late ones are never touched. A memory only survives metamorphosis if it happened to be written into hardware that wasn't scheduled for demolition.
