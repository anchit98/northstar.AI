#### The US is spending $22.5 billion to get rid of a cocktail lounge on wheels. That's what the Dulles People Mover actually was. In 1962, Chrysler built a fleet of rolling waiting rooms that raised themselves on hydraulics to dock with the airplane door. The original pitch was that you'd sip a drink in a moving lounge while your seat drove across the tarmac to the jet. The whole point was laziness as luxury. Dulles advertised that passengers walked 200 feet, total, from the terminal entrance to their airplane seat. No concourses, no gates, no hiking. The building came to you. It broke for a reason nobody in 1962 could see. United turned Dulles into a hub, and hub passengers connect from plane to plane without ever entering the terminal. The vehicle designed to eliminate walking turned into an extra vehicle wedged between two walks, loading 102 people through one door. Now the fix costs $22.5 billion. Four new concourses, an extended underground train, a walking tunnel. Dulles skipped building concourses for 64 years because the lounges made them unnecessary, and the entire deferred bill just came due in a single announcement. They were retired for being slow and weird. But for one decade, an airport tried to bring the bar to your seat, and we've spent the 60 years since walking further.

#### This book is 1,700 years old. The poem inside it was already 1,000 years old when a scribe in Egypt copied it down. It survives because of a format war. For most of antiquity the Odyssey lived on scrolls. A 12,000-line poem meant a stack of rolls, and finding one passage meant unrolling meters of papyrus in order. Around the 3rd century, readers switched to the codex, folded parchment bound into pages. You could write on both sides, halving material cost, and you could flip straight to Book 18. Random access, in 300 AD. You can still see the upgrade on this copy. The scribe wrote book numbers on the pages so readers could find their place. That is a table of contents being invented in real time. Then a second ancient reader went through it, fixed the scribe's mistakes, and added lines he'd skipped. Somebody 1,700 years ago loved this specific object enough to debug it. The reason it exists at all is weather. Almost everything ancient we can read survived in Egypt's dry climate, while Greece's humidity ate its own literature. In 1900 this manuscript's owner found the middle had rotted from damp. Every page now lives between two sheets of glass in Manchester. Every Odyssey you've ever read descends from a chain of hand copies like this one. One break anywhere in that chain, and the poem is gone forever.

#### Most PM job descriptions still ask for airtight PRD writing. The best AI teams have already stopped writing them. The reason is structural, not stylistic. A traditional PRD describes how a product should behave in specific situations. Gen AI products are expected to handle almost every situation, which makes that kind of document nearly impossible to write with any precision. The replacement is a pre baked set of trivia questions for the model, paired with an answer key that scores whether the response is actually good. Build a recipes app and instead of writing pages of edge cases, you write 100 prompts covering the ice cream flavors users might ask for, score the outputs, and use that offline set as a proxy for what real users will experience once it ships. @danielmckinn0n spent years as a PM on Llama at Meta and on Gemini at Google. He's been saying the same thing for two years now. Performance on that offline set is the best available signal for whether users will be satisfied. Score well offline, ship it, and let the online numbers confirm or deny the bet. The skill that used to separate strong PMs was writing a precise spec. Now it's writing a precise test. Most PM training hasn't caught up. Define the eval.

#### Pinned: A psychologist spent 10 years studying 400 people who called themselves lucky or unlucky. Then he handed each one a newspaper and asked them to count the photographs inside. Unlucky people took about two minutes. Lucky people took seconds. Page 2 held a half-page ad in giant type reading "Stop counting. There are 43 photographs in this newspaper." Feeling lucky does nothing in a game of pure chance, because there's nothing to notice. So he built a "Luck School" that trained unlucky people to behave like lucky ones. Participants reported lasting gains in fortune, confidence, and even health. The luck was sitting in plain sight on page 2, in type half a page tall. The only variable was who looked up.

- **Published:** 2026-07-29T18:43:03.000Z
- **Link:** https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/29/ai-worming-through-word/

AI Worming through Word Neat new prompt injection variant by Håkon Måløy, who found a way to upgrade prompt injection attacks against Microsoft Word to full self-replicating worms: An attacker places hidden instructions in a document that is later used as source material in Copilot for Word. Copilot may interpret those instructions as part of the user’s request, causing it to manipulate the document being drafted or edited. Copilot may then also copy the hidden instructions into the resulting document, turning that document into a new carrier. If the carrier is subsequently used in another Copilot-assisted workflow, the instructions can trigger again and propagate into further documents, even without the attacker’s original document being present. We've seen plenty of hidden white-on-white text before - the kids are using it in their job applications now - but this is the first one I've seen that deliberately copies instructions to self-replicate itself. It was responsibly disclosed to Microsoft who then had 144 days to work on a fix, but so far (unsurprisingly) there's no mitigation that covers the full class of attack.
